Does Alex Hormozi's Cold Calling Script Work for B2B? What 26,600 Dials Showed Us

We put Hormozi's cold calling advice up against 26,600 B2B dials. The parts we use every day, the parts that fall over on a VP of Sales, and our real pickup and meeting rates.

Matty Morgan Founder, Outbounda

Alex Hormozi’s cold calling script works well for B2B on list building and on volume. Parts of it don’t work on senior B2B buyers: the “act like you know them” opener and the free offer on the phone. A lot of his examples come from selling to gym owners, and a VP of Sales doesn’t respond to a cold call the way a gym owner does.

We’ve made around 26,600 dials at Outbounda between July and mid-October, calling for B2B SaaS and tech clients. We booked just under 200 meetings. A lot of Hormozi’s advice is what we already do every day. Here’s where we agree, where we do the opposite, and how our numbers compare with his.

Where Hormozi is right: build your own list

His point is simple. The cheaper and easier a list is to get, the more people have already called everyone on it. If a $99-a-month tool gives you unlimited leads, everyone else has those leads too.

That matters even more in B2B SaaS, because there are only so many companies that can buy from you. A gym owner who hangs up has another gym owner down the road. One of our clients sells software to robotics companies, and there are only a couple of thousand in the world they’d want to sell to. When the market is that small, you can’t afford to call off the same list as everyone else.

So we build every list ourselves and never buy ready-made ones. Two things from his playbook we use:

  • Test your data tools against each other. Pull a sample from each, check the contact info is current and the people are who the tool says they are, and buy more from whichever holds up. When one tool doesn’t have the number we need, we check the next, then the one after.
  • Build lists nobody sells. His version is joining groups and pulling members one by one. Ours is public records. For a visa and migration client we work from the government’s public register of accredited visa sponsors, almost 3,000 companies. Nobody sells that list, but anyone can find it.

Even on lists we build and check ourselves, up to 15% of the people who picked up were the wrong contact or had left the company. On a bought list, expect worse.

Where we do the opposite: the opener

Hormozi’s approach is to make a cold call feel like a warm one. Say their name, say your first name, then pause, so they search their memory for who you are. Don’t mention your company.

That might work on a gym owner. A VP of Sales has trained their own reps to do the exact same thing, so they’ll spot it in the first two seconds, and then they think you’re trying to trick them.

We do the opposite and say it’s a cold call in the first sentence:

“We’ve never spoken before. It’s a cold call, but a really well-researched one. Do you mind if I grab 30 seconds to explain why I’m calling?”

Across those 26,600 dials, even after telling people straight up that it was a cold call, upwards of 60 to 70% let us pitch and stayed on the phone for at least a minute.

He’s right that knowing something about the person buys you a few extra seconds. That’s why we research before we dial, and sometimes tell them that when they pick up.

Where it breaks: the free offer on the phone

Hormozi says the biggest breakthrough at Gym Launch was changing what they gave away for free on the phone. Offer something other people charge for, so the prospect already knows what it’s worth.

In a LinkedIn message that works well for us. Upwards of 25% of people regularly reply when the first message includes something we’d normally charge for.

On the phone, I haven’t cracked it. A gym owner can say yes on the spot because they own the place and free costs them nothing. A VP of Sales can’t. The free thing still eats their time, they probably need someone to approve it, and if it doesn’t work they’re the one who backed it. And the second you mention something free on a call, it sounds like the start of a pitch.

What does keep a senior buyer on the phone is a problem they recognise in the first 20 seconds. One of our clients sells managed IT services, so we call companies where we’ve found a mistake in how they set up their email security. The reason for the call is a problem in their own setup. The ask is small: 20 minutes to show what we found.

The name-drop voicemail

His voicemail mentions a company the prospect knows or competes with: “Calling in reference to Nike.” It gets callbacks, but the prospect may think Nike sent you.

In September I tested my version. I opened by mentioning that one of our clients had the same investor as them, something they could check with one text, without ever saying the investor sent me. From 80 dials, 11 people picked up, and every one of them gave me the minute easily. Some even told me where they were at with hiring salespeople.

Volume: right, with one catch

Hormozi says volume negates luck. If the best rep makes 200 calls, make 400. He’s right. On a calling day our reps make upwards of 250 dials each.

We get there by automating the dialling. When I was an SDR at Rippling I made around 100 dials a day, all manual. Now we use a power dialler, sometimes a parallel dialler, and Claude builds our calling lists in around two hours instead of one to two full days.

Where he loses me is giving up personalisation for scale. When deals are worth upwards of $20,000 a year, that’s where you start losing meetings. Tools like Clay let you personalise at scale now. On a call, the personalisation that helps most is something happening in the business right now, like a funding round.

Even Hormozi says that when there are only a few thousand buyers and each one is worth a lot, you go way out of your way. At Rippling, every rep picked 20 companies a month to send a good bottle of wine and a handwritten note. Around 70 to 80% turned into qualified opportunities. It was the least automated thing I’ve ever done.

Automate the list and the dialling as much as you can. Never automate what your reps say on the phone.

Our numbers against his

Hormozi’s example: 100 calls a day, 25% pickup, 25% of those take the lead magnet, so about four engaged leads a day, roughly one an hour.

Ours, July to mid-October:

Metric Result
Dials ~26,600
Pickups ~3,200 (about 12%)
Conversations ~2,000
Meetings booked ~200
Dials per meeting about 120 to 130

His four engaged leads said yes to a free thing. Our meetings are 20 minutes with senior buyers at companies our clients want to sell to. Different bar.

On pickups, across all our clients we see 12 to 15%. Our best sessions are around 17%, and we only reach 20 to 25% when targeting is perfect and we’re calling at the best times of day. So we do get his 25 pickups, but it usually takes 150 to 200 dials.

What a real calling day looks like

From July to October our reps had 95 days with 100+ dials. On those days they averaged:

  • 207 dials
  • 27 pickups
  • 17 conversations
  • 1.4 meetings

On 25 of those days they booked nothing. The best day was five meetings, and they booked three or more on 23 days.

Our best single session was five meetings in just under two hours, around 240 dials at a 17.3% connect rate, one meeting per 48 dials. That was one session calling finance leaders at e-commerce companies. Across every client and list, it takes nearly three times as many dials. As a rule of thumb, expect a meeting every 100 to 200 dials.

Track the numbers, then fix the right thing

Hormozi’s own story: twice he failed at cold outreach with people who didn’t track metrics. The third person did, and it worked.

Tracking tells you what to fix:

  • Fewer than 10% of calls get picked up? The list is the problem. No script change will fix it.
  • Fewer than 60% of pickups stay on the line? Your first 30 seconds is the problem.
  • Having conversations but booking fewer than 10% of them? You’re asking too early, or asking for too much.

Take his advice on lists, volume and tracking. Rework the opener and the free offer for buyers who’ve trained reps to do the same thing to them.
